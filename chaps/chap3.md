# III. Rationalism


Oakshott's critique of rationalism follows directly from the ideas he expressed
in *Experience and its Modes*. As my friend James Connolly once said to me in
conversation, Oakshott really only wrote two books: *Experience and its Modes* and
*Inexperience and its Modes*.

Some authors, failing to understand the connection between the earlier book and
the latter essay, actually wish to dismiss the book as some youthful aberration
and treat Oakshott's work from the 1950s on as though it were written by
Oakeshott 2.0. (See, for instance, Gerencser 2000.) To do so ignores the fact
that Oakeshott begins his essay "The Voice of Poetry in the Conversation of
Mankind" with a statement that the real world is a world of experience, or that
he opens his last book published in his lifetime, *On History*, by discussing the
different modalities in which we can view the past.

*Experience and its Modes* argues that we can experience the world through the
lenses of different presuppositions. Oakeshott calls these different sets of
presuppositions "modes" of experience. Although there is theoretical limit on
the number of modes that might exist, in that book, the chief modes discussed
are science, history, and practice. Later, in the "Voice of Poetry" essay, he
adds art as another, distinct mode. And in addition to the modes, Oakeshott
considers philosophy to be experience without presuppositions (as we saw in
chapter one).

A modal confusion, guilty of irrelevancy, occurs when a voice using the
presuppositions of one mode attempts to dictate discourse in a different mode.
An instance of such modal confusion would occur if artists were attempting to tell
scientists how to conduct their research based on aesthetic criteria.

But that particular example is unlikely in practice: what we see much more
commonly is someone using scientific, theoretical criteria to try to
dictate practice. (In Aristotelian terms, they are employing *theoria* when
what is called for is *phronesis*.) Examples of this sort of irrelevancy
abound: Dr. Spock telling parents how to raise children, "scientific" rules for
how to practice music, urban design based on theory rather than on the actual
experience of urban living (see Callahan and Ikeda, 2xxx), [MORE].
