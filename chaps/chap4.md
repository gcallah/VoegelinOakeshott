## Political Philosophy Versus Ideology

Besides agreeing on the basic nature of philosophy, Voegelin and
Oakeshott also share the view that political philosophy is clearly
distinguishable from political ideology.

For instance, Voegelin writes, "Plato was a philosopher, not a political
ideologist" (2001: 189).

For Oakeshott, as Fuller puts it, "To consider politics philosophically
is to describe the necessary character of politics in its endless
efforts to preserve and to change; it is not to prescribe courses of
action" (2024: 152).


## Ideolgies


### Liberalism

EV: "Liberalism itself changes in the process of history. It changes because it
is not a body of timelessly valid scientific propositions about political
reality, but rather a series of political opinions and attitudes that have their
optimal truth in the situation that motivates them, and are then overtaken by
history and required to do justice to new situations. Liberalism is a political
movement in the context of the surrounding Western revolutionary movement; its
meaning alters with the phase of the surrounding movement. Its field of optimal
clarity is the 19th century, which is preceded and followed by fields of
decreasing clarity, in which it becomes increasingly difficult to establish its
identity." (2000a, 85)

Voegelin understands classical liberalism as the attempt to stake out a middle
ground between the forces of reaction and those of the more radical left in
early 19th-century Europe. The Liberals of that time fought both against those
to the right, who wanted to restore the Ancien Regime, and those to its left
embracing revolution. But, he notes, the context changed so that "classical"
liberals like Raymond Aron and F.A. Hayek began to be called conservatives. And
in the United States, the term "liberal" came to mean "progressive," while the
term "conservative" means "liberal" in the European sense. And with Charles
Comte in France, there even developed the idea of liberalism as an ongoing
revolution, but one taking place through peaceful means.

Voegelin points out, revolutionaries like Trotsky have embraced the idea of a
permanent, violent revolution. And this permanence is a necessary feature of
their ideology: since its goal is the complete perfection of human society, and
that goal can never be reached, their revolution can never end.

And liberalism shares this problem with its more radical revolutionary brethren:

"For in liberalism also there is the irrational element of an eschatological
final state, of a society that will produce, through its rational methods,
without violent disturbances, a condition of everlasting peace. Liberalism too
is part of the revolutionary movement that lives to the extent it moves."
(2000a, 89)

 Voegelin goes on to analyze the work of Auguste Comte, who, as he shows, could
 be considered a liberal, a revolutionary, and a conservative all at once. He
 wished to completely overthrow existing religions with his new "Religion of
 Humanity." But he wished to propagate this religion through "a conservative
 organization. He wanted a temporal power of industrialists united with the
 spiritual power of intellectuals under the pontificate of Comte" (2000a, 90).
 Voegelin then points out the surprising fact that, "In view of later events,
 one could say that this is the model of an industrial fascism under the
 leadership of a gnostic sect" (2000a, 90).

Comte's contemporary liberals, such as John Stuart Mill, liked the "early"
Comte, since they appreciated his positivistic attack on metaphysics and
religion. But they were taken aback by his idea of establishing a universal
church of humanity with himself as that church's new pope. so they invented the
idea that there were two Comtes: an early one, one sane and liberal, and a later
one who had lost his mind.  [See Mises]

But Voegelin, citing Henri Gouhier, contends it has been demonstrated that the
messianic vision was there in Comte's work from the very beginning. The liberals
have been happy with Comte's attacks on theology and metaphysics, which seemed
to open the way for the replacement by "social physics." But "Comte knew,
however, that an imitation of the methods of natural science in the social
sciences is no substitute for spiritual order and its theological-metaphysical
symbolism." (2000a, 91)
