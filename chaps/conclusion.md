# Conclusion

We can now see how, despite their shared view of philosophy as "experience
without reservation or arrest," and their common admiration of Plato, Oakeshott
and Voegelin parted ways when it came to the practical value of philosophy. If
there *is* a *summum bonum* that humans can perceive, then philosophy can aid
us in identifying it, and orienting our lives towards it. But if our quest for
that highest good is a snark hunt, then pursuing it will only render our
practical lives a hollow effort to capture a phantom. And it is in their
differing evaluations of the work of Hobbes that we clearly see them moving
apart.

In his brief essay "Leviathan: A Myth," Oakeshott remarks that a civilization
is "at bottom a collective dream" (2000: 159). He continues, "What a people
dreams in this earthly sleep is its civilization. And the substance of this
dream is a myth, an imaginative interpretation of the human experience, the
perception (not the solution) of the mystery of human life" (2000: 159-160).
This understanding is very close to Voegelin's in *The New Science of
Politics*, where he notes that "Human society...  is a whole little world, a
cosmion, illuminated with meaning from within by the human beings who
continuously create and bear it as the mode and condition of their self
realization" (2000d: 109).  Indeed, Voegelin states that "At the border of
transcendence the language of philosophical anthropolgy must become the
language of religious symbolization" (2000b: 419), in other words, myth. And he
notes how, in the wake of the Macedonian conquests, the Greek poleis were
"societies that had lost their myth" (2000b: 422).  Oakeshott goes on to
describe the nature and novelty of the Hobbesian myth:

"The destiny of man is ruled by no Providence, and there is no place in it for
perfection, or even lasting satisfaction... To those brought up on the older
myth [that distinguished medieval Christian civilization], this will appear
an unduly disenchanted interpretation of the mystery of human life. But there
can be no mistaking its character.  It is myth, not science. It is a
perception of mystery, not a pretended solution." (2000: 162)

And he questions whether the Hobbesian myth is really as much a break with the
medieval Christian tradition as many perceived it to be:

"Pride and sensuality, the too much and too little -- these are the poles
between which, according to our dream, human life swings... If [the old myth]
erred, it was perhaps in a partiality for the too much... But in the myth of
our civilization, as it appears in *Leviathan*, the emphasis is on the
opposite pole; it recalls man to his littleness, his imperfection, his
mortality, while at the same time recognizing his importance to himself."


Thus, Oakeshott sees Hobbes as a shift in emphasis in the myth of our
civilization, whereas Voegelin perceives him as destructive of that myth.

So we can perhaps find these two great thinkers reaching a rapprochement in
their understanding that a civilization is grounded in myth. And then the key
question becomes, "Is the Hobbesian myth adequate to ground a civilization?" And
since the Hobbesian myth is the founding myth of liberalism, that question can
be re-phrased as, "Does liberalism offer a myth of its own sufficient to support
a civilization, or has its success been parasitic on the classical-Christian
myth it has attempted to supplant?"




It is our historical situation that we are confronted by a dilemma. Too often,
people will embrace one horn of the dilemma and dismiss those embracing the
other as deluded, while neither side truthfully acknowledges that there is
indeed a dilemma.

But to do so short circuits our ability to look squarely at the dilemma itself,
and come to understand exactly why it is a dilemma, and try to find a genuine
resolution.

Oakeshott and Voegelin, between them, made it clear just why we are in a
dilemma. Oakeshott believed that, given the sort of people we residents of
liberal democracies are, it is the appropriate form of government for us.
Voegelin told us that history, as the search for order, indicated that no
society lacking a shared sense of their place in a transcendent order will form
a stable secular order.

I suggest that both of them were right. Given the people we are, liberal
democracy is the right form of government for us. And it is inherently unstable.
And once one recognizes that both of those things are true, then it becomes
apparent why we oscillate between the poles of civil association and enterprise
association as a society. Both poles are attractive in their own way, but the
closer we get to one, the more attractive the other one becomes.  Civil society
is a useful mechanism for solving the problem of conflicts between citizens, but
as such it is no more deserving of our devotion than is the electric company,
because it is a useful way to get electricity to our homes.

Oakeshott would readily acknowledge that his clear preference for civil
association was dependent upon the historical circumstances in which he found
himself. He lived in a thousand-year-old nation, and he was imbued with its
traditions, customs, and lore. And he enjoyed that inheritance.


It does not require xenophobia to recognize that a society admitting vast
numbers of newcomers, unfamiliar with its ways, will experience increasing
disruptions in the smooth flow of social life. (Of course, even the most
tradition-bound society experiences disruptions, and even the least
tradition-oriented society must have some things smoothly working to continue to
exist.) And a likely consequence of this increase in disruptions will be a
demand for more laws to ease the friction.


Voegelin clearly saw this problem, but he did not offer us a solution, other
than pointing out that any stable social order had to embody an understanding of
the place of that society in "the beyond." That is because he recognized that
there is no rationalist plan that can engineer a people's way to such an
understanding of their place in the transcendent. Any such understanding can
only emerge from an historical process, the outcome of which no person in the
present can predict. [NOTE 1]

Contemporary liberals all too often ignore the fragility of our current
situation. Keeping our society on life support may be the best we can do at the
moment, but it is not a enduring arrangement. Liberalism is not the end state
towards which all previous human life has been aiming; it was the solution to a
historical situation, namely, the wars of religion that tore Europe apart in the
seventeenth century.

The post-liberal's vice is to leap from the fact that our current situation is
far from satisfactory, to conclude that it should be post-haste be replaced by
an arrangement more to their liking. The road ahead is dark and foggy, and while
we ought to have faith that we will be able to traverse it, if we truly aim at
the common good in doing so, it is presumptuous to think that we know exactly
where it will lead us.

Appreciating the fact that we really do live in a time where we are torn between
two poles of social organization is not a surrender; it is, rather, an
unflinching acknowledgement of our actual situation. if one is lost in the
woods, it is not a good idea to pretend that one is certain that one direction
or the other is the way out. If one were really certain, one would not be lost.



But both Oakeshott and Voegelin speak to us about why rationalist plans to avoid
confronting our dilemma can only make things worse. By attempting to ignore
Scylla, they embrace Charybdis, or to avoid Charybdis, they embrace Scylla.

instead, like the wily Odysseus, we must discover the best path between these twin dangers
