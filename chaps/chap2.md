# II. History

From his first book to his last, Oakeshott was concerned with the nature of
history. 

Philosopher and historian R.G. Collingwood, reviewing *Experience and Its
Modes*, wrote: "I can, in this brief notice, only say that it is the most
penetrating analysis of historical thought that has ever been written..." (1934,
249)

## The Past Is an Aspect of the Present

Both Oakeshott and Voegelin understand that the past of experience is the
present, viewed from a vantage point that looks at the present for survivals of
times now gone. (The survival we might find could include our own memories,
books describing past events, scars, physical structures, the ruins of physical
structures, coins, statues, artwork... in short, everything in the current
environment that can be understood as having survived from some previous
environment.

As Voegelin puts it:

"I do not remember something that lies 'in the past,' but I have a past because
I can make present a completed process of consciousness... And I do not project
'into the future,' but because I can project, I have a future. Past and future
are the present illuminative dimensions of the process in which the energy
center is engaged." (2002, 68)


## History Is *One* of Our Pasts


In 1935, Oakeshott wrote:

"History, as I understand it, is a certain way of thinking and of writing about
the past. There are many ways of thinking and writing about the past, but
history is separated from all others by certain distinguishing
characteristics... There is a past for every way of thinking." (2007, 129-130)

Compare to the beginning of On History.

Consider a memory of watching a solar eclipse with a loved one. From the point
of view of practice, one might ask the question, "The flight to Tahiti to see
that was expensive; was it worth it?" From an artistic point of view, one might
compose a sonnet about the experience. From a social scientific point of view,
the traveler might explore why the residents of Tahiti responded to the eclipse differently
than his date and he did. And an historical understanding of the event would
demonstrate it comprehensible place within a stream of historical events coming
before and or after it.

## The Telos of History


Voegelin:
"Is [a thinker] really entitled to hold a belief concerning the meaning of
history, although he perfectly well knows... that the meaning of history, its
essence or *eidos*, is unknown because history extends into the future and hence
is not a thing whose eidos can be known?" (2000a, 44)

## To Surrender to the Stream of History


Voegelin:
"When the theologian is victorious over the philosopher, there will be trouble
for the historian who now finds himself deprived of the conceptual instruments
for understanding "operative ideals." He cannot gain the necessary critical
distance from his object and must surrender to the stream of history. The
surrender does not make the work altogether worthless; while swimming with the
stream, one can still trace the course of events, assemble historical materials,
and discern the lines of meaning embedded in the evolution of the "operative
ideals" themselves." (2000a, 40)

But for Oakeshott, this "surrender to the stream of history" is exactly what
makes a work history proper, rather than the use of the materials of history
for some non-historical purpose.




EV: "A first answer to these questions could be the assumption that the history
of political ideas does not show an internal structure of meaning at all, and
that, as a consequence, the historian can do nothing but record ideas concerning
political problems in their chronological order." (2000e, 159)

EV: "The result would be not a history, if by history we understand the
unfolding of a pattern of meaning in time, but a chronological encyclopaedia."
(2000e, 159-160)
