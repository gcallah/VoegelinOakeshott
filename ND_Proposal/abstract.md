Oakeshott and Voegelin: The Quest for the Common Good

Gene Callahan
New York University

Dear Editors,

I am pleased to submit for consideration *Oakeshott and Voegelin: The Quest for
the Common Good*, a book-length comparative study of two of the most
distinctive political philosophers of the twentieth century, Michael
Oakeshott and Eric Voegelin.

The book begins from a simple observation: although Oakeshott and
Voegelin are usually encountered as political theorists, both understood
themselves to be philosophers, and both understood philosophy in a
broadly classical sense as an open-ended quest for understanding rather than the
construction of a closed intellectual system. Yet the two thinkers drew
strikingly different conclusions about what philosophy means for human conduct
and political order. For Voegelin, philosophy has an indispensable practical
dimension because the philosopher's quest for truth is inseparable from the
ordering of the soul and, ultimately, the possibility of rightly ordered
political life. For Oakeshott, by contrast, philosophy is radically
non-instrumental: philosophical understanding cannot provide a program for
practical conduct and becomes distorted when it attempts to do so.

The central argument of the book is that this divergence can be understood most
clearly through the two thinkers' contrasting interpretations of Thomas Hobbes.
Hobbes is not simply another figure whom both of them happened to discuss. He
represents a fork in the road. Voegelin regarded Hobbes as a decisive
representative of modernity's movement away from the classical and Christian
understanding of transcendence and the *summum bonum*. Oakeshott, while fully
aware of Hobbes's revolutionary character, regarded him as a thinker whose work
could help disentangle Christianity and political life from a
teleological conception of politics. The different meanings Oakeshott and
Voegelin assign to Hobbes consequently illuminate their different understandings
of liberalism, political philosophy, and the common good.

The book's contribution is therefore not simply to place two thinkers side by
side. It reconstructs a philosophical disagreement that has remained largely
implicit in the existing literature and shows how that disagreement bears on one
of the central questions of contemporary political thought: whether a liberal
political order can sustain itself without some conception of a substantive
human good. The book also argues that the disagreement should not be overstated.
Oakeshott and Voegelin share a strikingly similar understanding of experience,
philosophy, historical consciousness, ideology, and the dangers of intellectual
systems that claim to master reality. Their differences are
consequential precisely because in many ways they are so close.

The project builds on my previous work on Oakeshott and Voegelin. I am the
author of *Oakeshott on Rome and America* and, with Lee Trepanier, co-editor of
*Tradition v. Rationalism: Voegelin, Oakeshott, Hayek, and Others*. The latter
volume brought together comparative work on the critique of rationalism and
included Timothy Fuller's important treatment of Oakeshott, Strauss, and
Voegelin on Hobbes. The present book grows in part from the recognition that a
sustained comparison of Oakeshott and Voegelin themselves remains to be
undertaken. Indeed, a review of *Tradition v. Rationalism* specifically identified
a direct Oakeshott-Voegelin comparison as a desirable addition to the
literature. 

The manuscript is not a revised version of my doctoral dissertation. My
dissertation at Cardiff University concerned Oakeshott's thought on Rome and
America and was submitted in 2010; that work subsequently was publsihed as
*Oakeshott on Rome and America*.  The present book is an independent project,
although it naturally draws upon my long engagement with Oakeshott and
subsequent reading of Voegelin.

I currently have a substantial working manuscript, including the
introduction, seven chapters, conclusion, bibliography, and an earlier extended
paper (presented at the Michael Oakeshott Association conference but
unpublished) that develops the book's principal thesis. The manuscript requires
further development, expansion, documentation, and stylistic revision before
submission as a finished book. I anticipate completing the manuscript within
approximately twelve months of a publishing agreement.

I believe the book will be of particular interest to scholars and advanced
students of political philosophy, political theory, intellectual history,
liberalism, conservatism, philosophy of history, and the relationship between
religion and politics. It should also reach readers concerned with the
contemporary question of whether democratic and liberal institutions can survive
without an underlying account of the common good. That question gives the book a
natural connection to contemporary debates about democratic governance and the
social foundations of political order. In particular, there has been a flurry of
activity debating the idea of the common good of late, some of it prompted by
Vice President J.D. Vance's use of the term.
